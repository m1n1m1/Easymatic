# Messengers

### Messengers

WhatsApp, Signal and Telegram are **one trigger and three actions** — `trigger.message`, `action.reply_message`, `action.notification_action`, `action.send_message` — and **no vendor code anywhere**. None of those apps exposes an API to a third-party app on the same phone, so the whole integration runs over the one channel Android gives every app equally: the **notification**. A messenger that this has never heard of works on the day it is installed, which is why there is no package list to maintain and deliberately never will be. What identifies a message is the *shape* of the notification (`NotificationMessages`): a `MessagingStyle`, or a reply action. A title and a body is not evidence of a message, or the trigger would fire on a delivery update.

**Reading and answering are asymmetric, and that asymmetry is the design.** Replying is *silent and complete*: a messenger that supports watch or Auto quick-reply attaches an action carrying a `RemoteInput`, and filling that bundle in and firing the `PendingIntent` sends a genuine message with no UI — the app cannot tell it apart from a typed one because there is nothing to tell apart. Starting a **new** conversation has no silent path on any of them; the most the platform offers is the app opened with the text filled in and the person tapping Send. So there are two nodes with two different promises, and `MessageComposed.opened` is named `opened` rather than `sent` because that is the honest word. A macro that fires `action.send_message` at 3 a.m. leaves a chat window open and nothing sent.

**The handle is a live notification**, and it is the thing that surprises people: open the chat and the notification goes, and with it the only way to answer. `action.reply_message`'s KDoc says so, and the practical consequence is to put it *before* anything that takes time — a reply after a five-minute `action.delay` is a reply into a chat that has very likely been read. `AndroidMessaging` reports that case in words rather than as a cancelled-intent failure, and checks the package as well as the key: the platform reuses a notification key once its notification is cancelled, so a reference held across an `action.wait_until` could name a live notification belonging to something else entirely.

`ConversationRef` (`domain/model/`) is `msg:<packageName>|<sbn.key>`, split with a limit of **two, key last and unsplit** — `MailRef`'s shape for `MailRef`'s reason, with one ordering constraint of its own: a notification key is formatted `0|com.whatsapp|1|null|10123` and therefore *contains the separator*, where a package name cannot. It caches **no display name**, unlike `SmartHomeRef` and `PhoneRef`: those render in a config form long after being chosen, where this one is only ever wired, and the conversation title travels as its own field of `MessageEvent`.

**`trigger.message` is the one trigger that publishes a field of its event as a port of its own** — `conversationId`, beside the `message` struct, via `derivedOut` (`domain/model/Ports.kt`) and `TriggerNodeDefinition.extraOutputs`. It is a *projection* rather than a second computation, filled from the very value the struct port carries, so the two cannot disagree; `NodeDeclarationContractTest` pins that a trigger's data outputs are exactly its event plus its projections, which replaced the old "at most one data output" and is stricter where it matters — a declared port nothing fills is a socket that hands the consumer nothing. The general shape is deliberately **not** Unreal's split-struct-pin: expanding every field would put nine ports on the card. What earns a projection is a field that is *only ever wired* — the handle a sibling node takes, useless anywhere else — because there the `action.break` is pure ceremony. `trigger.mail`'s `ref` is the obvious second candidate and does not have one yet.

The naming across that pair is load-bearing rather than tidy. `MessageEvent.conversation` is what the chat is **called** and belongs in a notification; `conversationId` is opaque and belongs only in a wire. The trigger's output port, the reply node's input port and the config property all carry the one name `conversationId`, so the pairing is legible on the canvas — the first cut had a field called `ref` feeding a form row labelled "Conversation", which is the name of the *other* thing, and wiring the readable one in would have looked entirely reasonable and replied to nobody. `action.reply_message` prints back what it was given when a reference will not parse, because that mistake is the likeliest one and seeing "Anna" in the error is the whole diagnosis.

**A messenger updates one notification per chat rather than posting one per message**, which is invisible until it is missing and is what `MessageDedup` exists for. `onNotificationPosted` fires again whenever the app so much as refreshes the badge, re-delivering the same message; the question is therefore never "have I seen this notification?" but "have I seen it *showing this message*?", keyed on a fingerprint of the latest message. `onNotificationRemoved` forgets both the fingerprint and the reply handle, because a chat re-posted after an offline re-sync with the same last message must not be silently suppressed as a duplicate of something already dealt with. Group summaries (`FLAG_GROUP_SUMMARY`, the "3 messages from 2 chats" roll-up) and ongoing notifications (a call in progress) are dropped before any of that.

The trigger emits on its own `TriggerSource.MESSAGE` rather than filtering `NOTIFICATION`, so `trigger.notification` still sees every post exactly as it always did and one arrival can legitimately produce both. It is **not** stamped with the message's own time — that travels in the payload, where `TriggerEvent.timestamp` reads it, because `firedAtEpochMs` is what the bus expires a held event on and an hour of history posted at once would all look stale enough to drop on arrival.

**No `value.message`**, and the rule that asks for one exempts it: a message is an event with no resting value, the same exemption `trigger.sms` and `trigger.nfc` take. **No new permission**: `NOTIFICATION_LISTENER` already existed and all three notification-side nodes declare it, so `PermissionCatalogue`, `GrantedPrerequisites` and the Problems panel pick them up by derivation. `action.send_message` declares `LAUNCH_OVERLAY_PERMISSION` as the fourth member of that family.

`ActiveNotifications` (`data/notification/`) is a process-wide `object` on `VariableStore`'s reasoning — a `NotificationListenerService` is constructed by the *system* and can never be handed a dependency — holding the reply handles and the dedup state together because both are keyed by notification key and both die on the same `onNotificationRemoved`. It is bounded, which is not tidiness: each entry holds another app's live `PendingIntent`s. Cancelling goes through a lambda the listener registers rather than the service instance, so nothing can outlive the binding.

`MessengerLink` (`domain/model/`) is the compose-with-prefill table, pure and JVM-tested for `WebUrl`'s reason, producing a `MessengerRecipe` that lives in `core/service` because `SystemServices` consumes it and `core` may not import `domain`. The three apps genuinely differ: WhatsApp takes a `wa.me` URL with the number as international digits (a blank recipient is legal and opens the chat picker), Signal takes an `smsto:` intent with the body in an extra (a number is required — `smsto:` with none addresses nothing), and **Telegram cannot pre-fill text into a named chat at all**. There the text wins and `losesRecipient` says so, because a message with the wrong chat preselected is one tap from correct where a chat with no message in it is the whole job undone. The package is pinned on every one of them: a `wa.me` link is an ordinary https URL a browser would happily win, and an `smsto:` intent is one the phone's SMS app answers as readily as Signal does. Percent-encoding is written out rather than taken from `URLEncoder`, which writes a space as `+` — correct in a form body, and a literal plus sign in the middle of the message here.

**A national number is normalised before it reaches that table, and the phone is what normalises it.** Address books are full of numbers saved the way they are dialled at home — `0151 12345678` — and `wa.me` takes a country code and nothing else, so it reads `0151` as the country and opens a chat with nobody in it. That reads like the contact not being on WhatsApp rather than like a formatting problem, which is why it is worth fixing rather than leaving to be noticed. `SystemServices.toInternationalNumber` is a *platform* call and deliberately not a rule in `domain`, because the rule is not "swap the leading 0 for the country code": that is true across most of Europe, false in Italy where the 0 is part of the number, and meaningless in the NANP which has no trunk prefix. `PhoneNumberUtils` carries libphonenumber's table for every country; a hand-written version is the near-miss that messages a stranger. The region is the **SIM's** country and not the network's — `getNetworkCountryIso` follows the tower, so a German phone roaming in France would start reading its owner's own contacts as French numbers, and a macro that worked at home would message strangers on holiday. With no SIM at all the phone's region setting is the best remaining answer. What the platform cannot place is passed through **unchanged**, with `MessengerLink.looksNational` deciding whether the run log should say so — the alternative to reporting is guessing, and this is the one place not to guess. The messenger nodes are the only ones that do this: `tel:` and SMS handle a national number perfectly well, so `action.call` and `action.send_sms` are left alone.

The typeIds say **`message`, not `whatsapp`**, and that is not renameable later for the reason the light nodes' are not — a workflow persists the string and an older schema is discarded rather than migrated. The app names live in the *descriptions*, which `NodeSuggestions` searches, so palette search still finds them.
